# The Copy-editor’s Guide to Writing D&D

IF YOU WRITE D UNGEONS & D RAGONS CONTENT , YOU will probably want to write it in a way that follows the style of the
official books. For one thing, this style is going to be familiar to your readers, making it easy to follow. But more
importantly, you can take advantage of the care that the authors and publishers of the books have put in to develop this
style. Lots of subtle and clever decisions have been taken that help ensure the information comes across in a way that
is clear, concise, and as far as possible unambiguous. Luckily for us, Wizards of the Coast have released their style
guide and made it freely available at
https://www.dmsguild.com/product/267467/DMs-Guild-Creator-Resource--Style-Guide-Resources. But there is something of an
art to reading style guides, so if you find it hard work, this version may help to make it easier. In particular, it may
help to explain and expand on some of the key ideas behind the decisions in the guide.

Not everything is covered in this document and if you want the full details, you should still check the guide. However,
I have found that once the basic principles are clear, there is rarely any need to do that. Mostly it is easier to look
at actual examples in the books themselves. This document also contains some illustrations of formatted text to show you
some things to look for.

## How to Use This Guide

There isn’t really any perfect way to organize the material in this book, so after some initial comments about overall
style, I’ve just written a long list in alphabetical order. They’re listed in slightly different headings than the
official style guide, and I’ve grouped similar items together to keep things a little more concise.

## A Note on Versions

This guide only covers the current version of D&D, up to the adjustments made with the release of Mordenkainen’s
Monsters of the Multiverse. In recent months we’ve seen some preview materials suggesting the direction Wizards of the
Coast are taking with the books going forward, which will change some of the key concepts given here. However, these are
still in flux and will not become official for some time, so I have chosen to focus specifically on the current edition.
Some notes on how things appear to be changing are given at the end. Do I Have To? It isn’t a requirement to follow
these guidelines. If you prefer to go your own way, that’s entirely up to you. However, if you do decide to follow your
own style, you should make sure it is consistent. For example, if youprefer to write spells using initial caps, then
make sure you do it everywhere, not just in some places.

## The Basics

Before we get into the specifics of particular words and elements, we’ll start by looking at the general principles
behind the choices made. This should help you navigate through the fine detail later. Many of these ideas are not
specific to D&D, but generally apply to all non-fiction. You should follow them as far as possible.

### Organizing Information

It will help your readers if the information you are giving them is organized in a clear and easy-to-follow way. Keep
Sentences Short. Avoid long run-on sentences as far as possible. Each sentence should provide information about a single
idea, without moving on to the next one. That doesn’t mean your writing has to be a harsh staccato of four-word
sentences: you should still have some variety, but always stay focused on the information you are presenting.

**Use Paragraphs.** Just like a sentence, a paragraph should have its own identity. Each paragraph should contain
information that is logically connected together. If your paragraph is going on for more than a few lines, it is
probably time to move on to a new one and start a new thought.

**Use Headings.** Be generous with your use of headings, subheadings, inline headings (like the bold text at the start
of this paragraph), bullet points, tables, and all the other tools at your disposal to break text apart. This will help
readers navigate through your document. Except in exceptional circumstances, never go straight from a heading to a
subheading. After each heading, add some body text to explain what is to come under that heading.

**Never assume the reader has read the heading.** If the heading name includes key information, make sure you repeat
that information in the body text. For example: if the heading is the name of a subclass (“Paladin: Oath of Niceness”),
then the text should restate that name (“Paladins who swear the Oath of Niceness agree to be lovely at all times.”)

**Use Boxed-out Text With Care.** Boxed-out text can be a very handy tool but don’t use it too much, as it breaks up
your information flow. It should generally be possible to read the main text without reading the boxed-out text at all.

**Use Callouts.** When you place information in boxes, tables, or illustrations, make sure you refer to it somewhere in
the text where it is relevant. Avoid using words like “below” or “above” as they can be misleading and are easy to
accidentally mess up. Instead, give your items a title and refer to them by name. And if you can’t find a place in the
text where such a callout is appropriate, consider why that is. What is the purpose of the additional information?

### Voice

In grammar, we use the word “voice” to refer to the relationship between the subject of a sentence and its verb. But in
a more general sense we can use it to mean how we convey information in a sentence. **Stick to Active Voice.** Passive
sentences can cause problems when presenting information, as the “Sword of Ouch” box demonstrate. In general they are
best avoided. On the other hand, as in the preceding sentence, they still have their uses. Use Second Person. One of the
main uses of the passive voice is to create a sentence where the subject of the verb is unknown. (“Mistakes were made” –
but who made them?) A spell or magic item is a good example of this, which is why people are often tempted to use the
passive. When moving into the active we end up having to use long-winded expressions like “the caster” or “the person
wielding the sword”. D&D avoids this problem by switching to second person in this case, expressing spells, magic items,
and character class abilities in the “you” form (again, as shown in the boxed example). Use Gender-neutral It. When
talking about a creature of indeterminate gender, use the pronoun “it.” This includes humanoids, although you can use
“they” instead, especially for PCs. Use “they” or other preferred pronouns for nonbinary individuals. **Use Each instead
of All.** When describing something that affects groups, it is clearer to describe its effects on them individually.
Where possible, use “each” instead of “all” to focus on the individuals involved.

### Language

By default, D&D is written in US English. If you come from another English-speaking country, you could theoretically
choose to write in your local spelling and idiom, but it is probably best to follow the pattern your readers are used
to, especially because certain core terms like Armor Class are affected by this decision. If you are using
word-processing software then you can easily switch it to spell-check in US English, but if you are working in something
like Homebrewery or GM Binder that may be more difficult. The most obvious differences are the use of “armor, color,
odor”, and so on, rather than “armour, colour, odour”; and “center, meter, theater” instead of “centre, metre, theatre”.
Also, remember to use the -ize and -yze endings, rather than -ise and -yse, (a particularly common one is “paralyzed”).
In the US, words like “learned”, and “dreamed” are never spelled with a t at the end where they might be in other
varieties of English. Words like “defense” and “license” are spelled with an s, not a c, and verbs ending in an l like
“travel” do not get two ls when inflected but simply become “traveled” or “traveling.”

Here are a few additional pitfall words that might easily come up in fantasy role-playing: Axe (not ax, even though
that’s common in the US) Artifact (not artefact) Cozy (not cosy) Furor (not furore) Gray (not grey) Jail (not gaol -
although does anyone still write gaol?) Maneuver (not manoeuvre) Mold (not mould) Molt (not moult) Named for (not named
after) Sulfur, sulfurous (not sulphur)

Note also that in US English you should almost always use “toward”, “forward”, “afterward” etc, rather than “towards”,
“forwards”, “afterwards”. 2

## Core D&D Styles

In this part are examples and notes on how to lay out the main components of a D&D book.

### Body Text

An initial paragraph after a heading is written without an indent at the beginning, like this one. Subsequent paragraphs
should be indented. This includes paragraphs that follow lists, boxed text, and tables.

It is good practice to clearly separate text that describes what is happening in an evocative way (flavor text) from
text that gives instructions about dice rolls, damage, actions, and so on (mechanics). Where possible, put these on
different paragraphs, with the flavor text usually coming first.

### Punctuation

When writing lists connected by commas, use a comma before the final “and” or “or”, called a serial comma or Oxford
comma: “You can take this reaction any time you take fire, thunder, or lightning damage.”

Use double quotation marks with single quotation marks inside if necessary: “The world is called ‘Grayhawk.’” Use an
em-dash if you want to separate parts of a sentence—like this. Do not add spaces. Use an en-dash to join ranges of
numbers: “The information is on pages 22 – 24.” Generally avoid semicolons. Use lowercase after a colon: like this.

### Headings

D&D books feature three main levels of heading, not counting chapter titles, inline subheadings, or the captions used
for tables and notes. Level 1 headings, such as the heading “Core D&D Styles” at the start of this section, are large
and bold. Level 2 headings,such as “Headings” above, are smaller and underlined. Level 3 headingsare smaller still and
neither bold nor underlined. (NB: In Homebrewery and GM Binder, a level 1 heading is actually styled as “h2”, using
the ## markup, and this carries through to the other headings)

There is not an enormous amount of consistency in how these headings are organized. For example, in class descriptions,
main class features tend to be listed under level 2 headings, but subclass features will appear as level 3 headings. In
all but a small number ofcases, as long as you use the headings in a strict hierarchy, with level 2 headings appearing
under level 1, and level 3 under level 2, it will look fine.

### Inline Subheadings

An inline subheading is a piece of text used at the start of a paragraph to describe the paragraph’s contents. These may
be in italics, bold, or both. They are usually followed by a punctuation mark: nearly always a period but occasionally a
colon. The punctuation mark should be styled in the same way as the subheading. Bold Italic Paragraphs. This subheaded
paragraph within a description body uses bold italics. These are also used unindented in stat blocks for actions and
abilities.

**Bold Bullets.** Because this is a bullet-pointed paragraph, it uses plain bold.

**Hanging Indents:** Indented paragraphs of this kind are often used in lists of data, such as at the top of spell
descriptions or in lists of class proficiencies. The header is usually bold, often using a colon rather than a period.

**Numbering.** Numbered lists are rare, but sometimes found in stat blocks, such as for the eye rays of a beholder. In
this case they use italics for the inline header.

### Tables

Tables should have a title and a header row. If a table is numbered for a die roll, write the die roll on the column
header, rather than the words “Die roll”. Center-align columns if they contain only numbers, otherwise left-align.

### Spells and Magic Items

Spells and magic items use a very similar visual language, with an initial title followed by additional information in
italics. As already mentioned, the spell or item description is writen in second person.

### Monsters

As far as possible, monsters in a bestiary should be listed in strict alphabetical order. The practice in the

Monster Manual of listing by category has been mostly discontinued. One exception is for monsters whose category is part
of their name, such as “Green Abishai,” “Red Abishai,” “White Abishai,” all listed under “Abishai”. Physical
descriptions of monsters, along with details of their behaviors, habits, and preferences, should all be listed outside
the stat block, which should be reserved for traits that affect combat. In the Monster Manual these were broken up into
paragraphs with inline subheadings, but these have been removed in Monsters of the Multiverse, with only a few large
blocks placed under level 2 headings.

### Abilities and Skills

Strength, Dexterity, and so on are “abilities” and should always be written with an initial capital. They can be
abbreviated to Str, Dex, etc; do not write these in caps. Athletics, Acrobatics, Perception, and so on are “skills” and
should also be written with an initial capital. In most cases they should be written in parentheses, with the relevant
ability beforehand. The word “passive” in a passive ability score should not be capitalized. “You must make a DC 14
Dexterity saving throw.” “A character that succeeds on a DC 15 Charisma (Deception) check can bypass the guards.” “A
character with a passive Wisdom (Perception) score of 15 or more notices the trap.”

### Actions

Action names should always be written with an initial capital. Most actions are verbs, but attacks are usually given by
the name of the weapon used. “You can Disengage as a bonus action.” “The boar makes a single Tusk attack.”

### Advantage and Disadvantage

These terms apply to rolls, not events. “You make your next attack roll or saving throw at advantage.” “The target has
disadvantage on all saving throws until the end of its next turn.”

### Alignments

Alignments such as chaotic good should be in lower case.

### Book Titles

When referring to other books, use italics. In official books these are almost always written out in full—many other
authors do abbreviate them for brevity, but restrict this to short notes. Note that Player’s Handbook and Dungeon
Master’s Guide both contain apostrophes and the word “the” is not part of their title. For books credited to
individuals, include the name if it is part of the main title, but not if it is listed as “presents”: Tasha’s Cauldron
of Everything, but Monsters of the Multiverse. “The patch of sand is quicksand (see “Wilderness Hazards” in chapter 5 of
the Dungeon Master’s Guide).”Can and May “Can” is used to refer to something you have the ability to do, whereas “may”
is used to refer to something you are permitted to do. Many of us were conditioned at school to use “may” all the time,
but “can” is often better, and should be used in almost all cases in D&D. “You can use this ability a number of times
equal to your proficiency bonus.”

### Capitalized Terms

These terms are almost always abbreviated, but when not abbreviated, use initial caps. Armor Class (AC) Challenge Rating
(CR) Difficulty Class (admit it, you forgot that’s what DC even stands for) Experience Points (XP)

### Classes

Character classes such as cleric, paladin, and wizard are written in lowercase.

### Conditions

Conditions such as prone, paralyzed, or unconscious are written in lowercase.

The frightened condition specifies that your fear must have a source. You cannot just be frightened, you must be
frightened of something. “The target creature must succeed on a DC 14 Wisdom saving throw or be frightened of its
nearest ally.”

### Creature Types

Creature types should be capitalized when used in mechanics, but not in regular text. “A creature of type Undead takes
half damage.” “The area is haunted by ghosts and other undead.” Cross-References Use lowercase to refer to chapters,
pages, appendices, etc—but avoid page references unless absolutely necessary. Refer to sections and subsections by name
instead.

### Damage Types

Use lowercase for damage types (poison, thunder, etc).

### Death

“You are dying” means you are unconscious and in a state where death saving throws are needed. “You die” means no
further death saving throws are possible and you can only be revived by magic.

### Distances

Use “feet” in most cases, except for movement speeds, viewing distances, and attack ranges where you can use “ft.” Even
for these, it is better to write the word out in full except in stat blocks. Do not use an apostrophe, as in “50’ high”
(this used to be standard in older versions of D&D and is a common error among older writers!)

Remember to hyphenate distances when used as adjectives. “The giant’s movement speed is 40 feet.” “Melee Weapon Attack:
+5 to hit, reach 5 ft.” “The dragon breathes fire in a 50-foot cone originating on itself.” “The torch emits light in a
50-foot-diameter sphere.” “The room has a 30-foot-high ceiling.”

### Effect Duration

When describing the length of an effect, do not use expressions such as “for one round” but explicitly say on which turn
the effect ends. “The target is poisoned until the end of its next turn.” “You can repeat the saving throw at the start
of each of your turns, ending the effect on yourself on a success.”

### Formulas

Use spaces between numbers and operators in formulas: “2d6 + 4”, not “2d6+4”. Use correct mathematical symbols: + − ÷ ×
. Do not use an x instead of a multiplication sign, and be aware that the minus sign is its own symbol distinct from a
hyphen or en-dash (although frankly no one will notice the difference).

Give average values for rolls in monster stat blocks and when describing the outcomes of traps and other hazards. Do not
give them for spells, magic items, or player abilities.

### Hit Points and Hit Dice

Hit Dice should be capitalized but hit points should not (for some unaccountable reason). Do not use the abbreviations
HP, HD, hp. When taking damage, do not mention hit points, just say the number and the damage type. “You take 1d6
bludgeoning damage.” “You lose 2d6 hit points.” “You regain 10 hit points.” “You gain 10 temporary hit points.” “Your
hit point maximum increases by 1.”

### Languages

Capitalize languages: Common, Elvish, Undercommon.

### Levels

Do not use superscript for ordinals (1st, 2nd).

Remember to hyphenate levels when used as an adjective. “You gain a feat when you reach 4th level” “Magic missile is a
1st-level spell”

### Magic Items

Names of normal magic items should be written in lower case and italics, except for proper names: Keoghtom’s ointment.
Don’t italicise in treasure tables, or when just using a part-word from the item, such as the word “ointment” on its
own. Artifacts, as individual magic items rather than members of a whole class, use title caps and take a definite
article, which is not itself italicised: “the Axe of the Dwarvish Lords“.

### Rarity.

Magic item rarity levels (common, rare, etc) are not capitalized.

### Money

Money should be abbreviated to gp, ep, pp, etc. Use spaces, and do not hyphenate eg “a 50 gp ruby”.

### Monsters

A monster’s name should be written in bold when the monster is first mentioned in each location, in the form it appears
in the Monster Manual. If it comes from some other source book, make that clear in the text. “The room contains three
black puddings.” “The giants’ farm holds thirty giant turkeys (use the axe beak stat block).” “A cranium rat (see
Monsters of the Multiverse) is hiding behind the toilet.” “A crystal golem (see appendix A) guards the door.”

### Nonmagical

“Nonmagical” weapons, not “non-magical”.

### Numbers

Separate thousands with commas. Use en-dashes to separate ranges of numbers. Write numbers in words if they are small
and appear within descriptive text, but always use numerals for mechanics. “If a monster has CR 30 (155,000 XP) it
should deal 303 – 320 damage per round” “The journey takes five days.” “The spell has a duration of 5 minutes.”

### Party

A party is a singular thing, but made of individuals. Use singular verbs to describe things the party does as a whole,
but you can use plural verbs to describe things they do separately, if it feels more natural. “The party discovers a
hidden cave.” “When the party have all finished investigating the cave, they leave.” 9

### Percent

Write in full: “a 50 percent chance”, not “a 50% chance”

### Plus and Minus

Except in formulas, write in full: “4 piercing damage plus 12 poison damage”

### Reactions

A reaction has to have a trigger: you need to react to something. “Any time you take damage, you can use your reaction
to make a single melee attack against a creature within range.”

### Saving Throws

There are a number of different issues to take into account when talking about saving throws. Make v. Succeed. A
creature “makes” a saving throw when it rolls the dice, irrespective of the outcome. Do not use “make” as a synonym for
“succeed”. Save First, Effect After. Don’t use, for example, “The character is knocked prone unless they succeed on a DC
14 Dexterity saving throw.” Start with the save, then describe the result of success or failure. Save or Suck. For
saving throws that have a reasonably standard outcome, which only occurs on failure, you can use either “… must make a
successful DC 14 Dexterity saving throw or …”, or preferably “… must succeed on a DC 14 Dexterity saving throw or …”
Complex Saves. If the saving throw has complex outcomes, or different outcomes depending on success or failure, break it
up into separate sentences or clauses. “The target must succeed on a DC 16 Constitution saving throw or take 2d6 poison
damage and be poisoned until the end of its next turn.” “The target must make a DC 16 Constitution saving throw, taking
2d10 poison damage on a failed save or half as much damage on a successful one.” “The target must make a DC 16
Constitution saving throw. On a failed save it takes 2d10 poison damage an is poisoned until the end of its next term.
On a success it takes half damage and is not poisoned.”“Half damage on a success” is an acceptable variant for “half as
much damage on a successful one.”

### Sizes

Capitalize size classes (Tiny, Small, Medium, etc)

### Skills

See “Abilities and Skills.”

### Spaces

“stat block”, not “statblock” “hit points”, not “hitpoints” “DC 10”, not “DC10” “CR 1”, not “CR1”

### Spells

Spell names are almost always written in lowercase and italics, other than proper names: Tasha’s hideous laughter. The
only exception is in class spell lists (such as at the start of Chapter 11 of the Player’s Handbook), where they are
written in roman, with initial caps: Tasha’s Hideous Laughter. Spell Range. Beware of the difference between a spell’s
range and its area of effect. The range determines the center point for an area of effect spell, so a spell that is
centered on the caster has a range of Self. If it has a circular area of effect, that can be written as “Self (30-foot
radius)”. If it has another area of effect such as a cone, the range is Self and the details are written into the body
of the spell. Spell ranges are capitalized.
